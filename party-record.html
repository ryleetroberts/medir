Meredith’s theory of Echoes and Numen sites

Spoken

Gilded Archive, and again at her map with PelleasAmbertide 23–24 and Solargold 10–11

What Vivid Echoes are, in her theory. Echoes appear most often at places with a great deal of “memory power,” the deep impression left where significant human experience happened: battles, coronations, festivals, treaties. Orrin’s Square in Brighthold, with a thousand years of continuous significance, rates a 7 on her scale and fits the theory, as do most Echoes.

Where the theory breaks. Some of the strongest Echoes appear at sites which have no recorded history to explain them. The standing-stone sanctuary and cave south of the Merchant’s Road has no recorded events, settlements, or ceremonies, and no one remembers what culture built it, yet its Echo rivals sites of known importance. She asks what the Echo is responding to when no one alive remembers what happened there.

Numen Sites. Two sites register at 12 or 13 on a scale she says should not go that high. She coined the term “Numen Site” for places whose memory power is so concentrated and so ancient that it would have to dwarf anything human activity could produce. She concludes they hold power from something that predates the accepted historical narrative. She also found evidence that something was actively preventing her from understanding these sites, and suspects there are more, better-hidden ones.

  • The Harmonwood in the Violet Spires’ holdings, is open only to the Council of Archmagi. Its accumulation spans multiple millennia, past the earliest recorded history and past the invention of writing.

  • The Crucible in Solrest’s interior is a pre-unification proving ground for Solresti warriors, protected “by fear.” A Solresti merchant told her caravans route around it, “adding five days to our journey.”

Faithful of the Flame-Crowned Herald's journal

Journal

A sealed tin in a satchel beneath the best bedroll in the camp on The TeethStormcrest 15–20

Written in Common in a hand that starts careful and deteriorates. Some pages are illegible.

Early entries: Marek was hired in Shimmerport with a group of others—guides, porters, a few sell-swords, and a cartographer. Standard desert contract, good pay, no questions. He notes the employer never showed his face during the hiring. The work was arranged through intermediaries—you realize from the descriptions that they’re the Ten-Thousand Colored Eye cultists, though Marek didn’t understand that based on his writing. The first time he met the man in charge was two weeks into the desert travel, when their escort delivered them to a forward camp. Marek describes him only as “tall, regal, with hair made of flames.” He notes that the man spoke Common with an accent he couldn’t place, and left with his retinue ahead of their group.

Middle entries: The company traveled west, away from Anmar’s Gift, into open desert with no landmarks. Marek knew this stretch—he’d guided caravans near the edges of it—but they pushed deeper than he’d ever gone. The air changed and got hotter, even moreso than normal desert heat. Then the sand started to change color, as if it was being burnt by roasting in the intense heat. He writes about his chest feeling tight, about a smell like hot iron and old blood. The animals refused to go further once the sand started turning hard, like it was baked clay. The horses screamed and had to be blindfolded and led. One of the mules bit through its lead rope and bolted back into the desert.

Late entries: The group split. Most continued toward what Marek now calls The Crucible—he picked the name up from the soldiers. Marek and a small party were sent to what you realize is The Teeth to find and prepare a supply route. The Herald, as Marek now calls him, wanted a way to avoid having to go through Bramwater or the mountains, hopefully shortening lead times. The last few entries are short. The weather turned. Two of the party died on the third island—exposure and a fall. Marek and the only sellsword left both ran out of water on the fourth.

The dead man’s notebook

Journal

Old fighting pit outside Corsair’s ReachEmberfall 9–13

A middle-aged man in road-worn clothes lay dead in the alcove behind the feeding hatch, struck across the chest and throat by something with large sharp claws. He wore a brass pendant of an open eye inside a chain-link circle. His satchel held a notebook in Common.

Early entry: The Echo opened, above the old arena, just like I thought it would. The feeling was the same as the last time—the pressure behind the eyes, the hum in the jaw. The Eye sees what we cannot. What came through this time was unlike anything I have encountered. Six different creatures of three different species. Large. Powerful. Angry. I must take time to study them.

Mid-journal: Of the three types of beasts, I have found the large beasts with the horns to be the most amenable. The larger of the two is the most docile, and although it will not eat from my hand, it recognizes that when I approach the bars of its cage that means food is coming, and stands an appropriate distance away. These beasts seem to accept raw meat in addition to roughage.

I believe these beasts were kept in cages and darkness for a long time before arriving in our world. At times, they rampage in their holdings, and I presume it is the fury of something caged that has been caged again, and I am sorry for that. I have explained to it that this containment is temporary, although I know it doesn’t understand me. When the Eye opens fully, there will be no more cages.

Later: I have been thinking about selection. Why these three? The Eye does not act on randomness. It selects. I am more certain of this now than when I began my research years ago. All three bear the marks of prior captivity, such as scars from restraints and behavioral patterns consistent with creatures trained to fight on command. They were weapons in whatever world they came from. They were used.

For these fighting beasts to be put into a fighting arena in Medir goes beyond mere coincidence. This, in combination with the more stable signature of the Vivid Energy surrounding them, leads me to believe that the entities brought to Medir possess a stronger physical tether to our realm under the correct circumstances. I must verify this hypothesis—I will petition the Herald to open another Echo for observation. This evidence won’t satisfy her, however, so I’ll need to gather more before I can return.

Final: The tall, insectile ones are more intelligent than I first believed. The one with the greener skin watches me the way I’ve been watching it. It even seems to be studying the Echo at times. It is learning my routines. I think it understands that I feed it. I do not think it is grateful.

I must repair the partition on the western pen. The feline beasts have been testing it.

The Grove of Song

Vision

Ambertide 21–22

The crystal shows a moonlit grove. A young gnome woman stands before a circle of gnome children, holding a scepter of polished hazelwood wound with fine copper wire in intricate patterns. The “notes” in the musical staff patterns are actually tiny carved hummingbirds, and the scepter hums with impossible melodies. She has Fizz’s distinctive eyes, nose, and chin—the family resemblance is unmistakable.

Eldera: “Magic isn’t chaos, children. It’s a song trying to teach us harmony. The wild surges, the unpredictable flares—that’s just the melody finding its rhythm through us.”

She raises the scepter, and reality seems to bend around the music it produces. Flowers bloom in fast-forward, then crystallize into gemstones. The air shimmers with colors that have no names.

Children: “Yes, Miss Eldera!”

You realize that the magic Eldera is demonstrating predates known spell structures. It’s as if she’s not accessing magic at all, but producing it as a font of pure magical energy itself.

Eldera: “We are not masters of this force. We are its instruments. And every instrument must learn to resonate with the greater symphony.”

As the lesson concludes, an adult gnome approaches through the trees.

Adult Gnome: “Miss Thistlewood?”

Eldera: “Yes, what is it?”

Johnhale and the Forgotten Nine

Meredith’s research on Johnhale and the Consilium Memoriae

Spoken

Gilded ArchiveAmbertide 23–24

Johnhale Goldfinch was a lawyer with an apparently perfect memory who began to "remembered things that never happened” and had “contradictory versions of the same events.” The breakdown came in 1448 AU, the Vividness year. The family disowned him and he vanished from every record within months, with no death certificate and no grave marker in the family plots. Fragments say he founded “some kind of monastery or scholarly retreat” after his exile.

The Consilium Memoriae stores a memory so it can be “experienced rather than just read.” A low-profile sect of The Scholar’s followers makes them. One reference says the sect believes “what is remembered cannot be unmade.” Meredith finds no official connection to Johnhale and calls the timing of the crystals and his “monastery of memory” “suggestive.”

Atticus’s father’s letter

Letter

Delivered by courier, Goldfinch crestAmbertide 23–24

Atticus,

Word has reached us of your involvement in the Festival incident and your work with the Crown. Your mother worries, naturally, but I confess I am... cautiously pleased. This is meaningful work, the sort that matters.

The Archive Covenant’s four verses

Verse

A lectern book in the Sage’s RestSolargold 22–23

Ilya i Ulmë (I. The Void)
First, know this: there is a void where knowledge once lived. Not emptiness that never held anything, but absence—the shape of what was taken. This is the most dangerous lie: that what is missing was never there at all. The darkness speaks loudest in what it refuses to echo.

Ilya i Gwath (II. The Shadow)
What was stolen does not simply vanish. It persists in shadow and whisper, in dreams half-remembered and words that catch in the throat. Truth denied still echoes. The faithful keeper knows: what endures in twilight cannot be fully extinguished, only obscured. And obscurity is not erasure.

Ilya i Ithil (III. The Moon)
Against the void stands memory—fragile as moonlight on water, yet persistent as the tide. We who preserve do not serve ourselves but those yet to come. Each record kept, each truth spoken, each fragment saved is a silver thread woven against forgetting. This is the covenant: to remember when others cannot, will not, dare not.

Ilya i Anor (IV. The Light)
The burden of preservation is this: truth revealed is truth endangered. Yet still we must bring it forth into light, for knowledge kept in darkness serves nothing. Like sunrise burning away the night, we must illuminate what was hidden—not for glory, but for necessity. The golden duty of the keeper: to remember is not enough. One must also reveal.

Record of the student Kaisuo, set down and sealed by order of the assembled masters, 1473 AU. On the night of the Trial of Resonance the student Kaisuo fell to lightning that belongs to no modern Path. Before his fall the masters had judged him the finest student of Ka in living memory, and several among us believed his flame would carry him further than any practitioner of this generation, perhaps of this millennium. His heart cannot be heard, his breath cannot be seen, and his wounds do not worsen or heal. He does not wake, does not age, and does not decline. Yet, he is alive. The Sui rites of restoration found nothing in him to restore, and the Ku rites of passage found no one departing. It is the judgment of this record that something reached him as he fell and set him aside from time, and that no hand within these walls did it. The gates are sealed and his condition entrusted to silence. He is kept in the far room of the sanctum, tended by Master Omashi and watched in shifts. May he open his eyes among us, or be released in peace, and may whoever holds his thread be a friend to him.

Private assessment of the student Raiten, kept by Master Retsujirō, 1473 AU. He was left at our gates in the dead of winter with no clan, no sigil, and no letter. His lightning is not a failure to find a Path. I believe it is older than the modern Paths. I have overseen students losing hold of a technique for over a century, and this was not that. In my fear, I called him a calamity to the masters in private. And after the Trial between Raiten and Kaisuo, I did so once more before the assembled students. However, my folly is letting fear of a prophecy control me; as the Venerable Master Renjiro of the Ku path reminded me, no one but Ku itself may know for certain what a prophecy means.
